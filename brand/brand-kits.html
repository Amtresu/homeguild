The HomeGuild · Brand kits · August 2026

Three ways to look like the list nobody can buy their way onto.

The HomeGuild is a private register of vetted home professionals for Treasure Valley homeowners. Local competitors sell leads to contractors; the Guild is paid by homeowners and answers to them. Each kit below carries that reversal differently. All three are already built into the site — switch between them with the pill at the bottom of any page.

Kit A

The Register

A civic ledger. The brand behaves like a county record book: ruled paper, ink the colour of the Boise River at depth, a copper seal for admissions and a verdigris one for "vetted". Nothing is decorated; everything is entered.

Newsreader carries the headlines at book weight, with one italic word per headline as the only flourish. IBM Plex Mono sets every number, member ID, and label — the typographic signal that this is a record, not a brochure.

"Listed by number, not by who paid."

Voice: plain, procedural, a little dry. Short declaratives. Numbers wherever a claim can be counted. Never "premium", never "curated".

Choose this if

You want the brand to read as an institution from day one — something closer to a title company or a county clerk than a startup. Ages best of the three.

Watch for

Square corners and hairlines can feel severe on mobile; the copper italic is doing a lot of the warmth. Photography, when it arrives, should be documentary, not styled.

Kit B

Basalt & Sage

The Valley itself. Snake River basalt for the ground, chalk for the ink, bitterbrush gold for the one thing that matters on each screen, and sage for the "vetted" mark. Contour lines of the Boise Front run behind the hero — the only ornament, and it is a map, not a pattern.

Bricolage Grotesque set tight and heavy gives the headlines a trail-sign directness; Public Sans keeps the body neutral so the grotesk stays the only loud voice. Dark-first because the register is a place you go into, not a page you skim.

"Two doors in. Neither is wide."

Voice: direct, a bit terse, proud of the place. Sentences that would fit on a trailhead sign. Comfortable saying no.

Choose this if

You want the brand to be unmistakably from here, and to look strong on a phone at night. Best of the three for social and for the eventual member app.

Watch for

Dark-first sites need discipline: any stock photo or bright partner logo will fight the basalt. The gold membership block is the one loud moment — keep it the only one.

Kit C

Ironwork

A craftsmen's guild, literally. Linen ground, forge-black ink, oxblood for the accent, brass for the seal. The mark is a gable with a keyhole in it: a house you need a key for. Buttons wear a double rule, like a stamped plate.

Libre Caslon at display and text sizes is the only fully-serif kit — the body reads like a bound bylaws book. Barlow Condensed does the labels and buttons in hard uppercase, the way a maker's mark is struck into metal.

"No firm has ever bought its way onto this page."

Voice: formal, slightly old-fashioned, unapologetic. Full sentences. "Firm", "admitted", "nomination", "bylaws". Reads like it was written by the people who will call your references.

Choose this if

You want to lean hard into "guild" — heritage, craft, a membership card people would keep in a wallet. Strongest for print, signage, and the physical seal on a member's door.

Watch for

An all-serif body is the least neutral choice and the slowest to read on small screens. Oxblood and brass together can tip toward "steakhouse" if photography goes warm; keep imagery cool and grey.

Recommendation

Go with A, and borrow B's contour lines for the member app later.

The Register is the only kit whose every choice restates the business model: it looks like a record because it is one. It gives you the institutional trust an application-only product needs on day one, and its warmth comes from the type rather than from colour, so it will survive whatever photography you end up with. Basalt & Sage is the better-looking screenshot and the right call if the brand will live mostly on phones and social. Ironwork is the most distinctive but the hardest to keep from tipping into costume.
